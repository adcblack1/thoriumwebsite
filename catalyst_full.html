ENTERPRISE

California's AI Tool Worked Fine. They Killed It Anyway.
Share X in

California has spent at least $5.8 million on AI pilots for state government. Half of them are already dead.

In 2023, Gov. Gavin Newsom signed an executive order launching eight generative AI projects across state agencies, from a call center assistant for tax questions to a tool meant to predict recessions. The pitch was efficiency without cutting services. Roughly a year and a half later, four of those projects are gone — including one that was actually working.

The California Department of Tax and Fee Administration paid $445,000 for a custom AI call center tool built by SymSoft Solutions. Powered by Anthropic's Claude, it listened in on employee calls with taxpayers, searched more than 16,000 pages of tax policy, and suggested answers in real time. According to the department's own assessment, it reduced average handle time and freed up staff. Then the department killed it — not because it failed, but because off-the-shelf tools from AWS had caught up to what SymSoft built custom, and the department already had an AWS contract. Paying $445,000 for a bespoke tool when the generic version does roughly the same thing stopped making financial sense.

That's a dynamic a lot of organizations are about to run into. The tool didn't fail. The market just moved faster than the vendor could justify its price. The other three kills were more straightforward: funding dried up, the agency decided to build internally, or the pilot never got off the ground.

Four projects survived, though the reviews are mixed:

+ Caltrans has the biggest spend at $3.5 million across contracts with Deloitte and Accenture for traffic management and road safety analysis. Both ongoing.

+ The Department of Finance has a $565,000 contract with Authorium for a tool that analyzes legislation and estimates costs. One tester said it "misses a lot of things or has inaccuracies, so it still needs to be double-checked."

+ California Health and Human Services is in a two-year, $1.3 million contract with Smartling for AI-assisted translation.

The state workers' union isn't enthusiastic either. SEIU Local 1000 president Anica Walls said members have reported AI tools actually adding to their workload, and that "these AI programs can't handle the complexity of the public's needs."

That 50% survival rate sounds rough until you look at the private sector. A MIT report covered by Fortune found that 95% of generative AI pilots at companies fail to deliver ROI. California beat that curve by a wide margin — and it did it by doing the thing most organizations struggle with, which is actually pulling the plug. The CDTFA kill is going to be the most common AI procurement story of the next 12 months. Custom vendors are racing against platform players who keep building the same capabilities directly into AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud. Knowing when to cut a working tool might end up mattering more than knowing which tool to buy in the first place.

WORKFORCE

JPMorgan Built an AI Scoreboard for Its Engineers
Share X in

JPMorgan Chase is keeping score on which of its engineers use AI.

The bank built internal dashboards that track individual usage of AI coding tools across the company, and they're already live. Of nearly 70,000 employees given access to GitHub Copilot, only about 24,000 show up as active. Global CIO Lori Beer said the productivity gains were significant enough that the internal conversation shifted from whether engineers should use AI to why they aren't all using it.

The numbers back that up. JPMorgan's AI tools have boosted engineer productivity by 10% to 20%. CEO Jamie Dimon told Bloomberg this week that the bank will be "hiring more AI people and probably less bankers in certain categories," calling current AI use "the tip of the iceberg." The company's tech budget is approaching $20 billion this year, up roughly $2 billion from 2025.

Every major bank on Wall Street is pushing AI adoption hard right now — Citigroup made training mandatory for 240,000 employees, Bank of America says AI now handles 30 billion client interactions, and Morgan Stanley estimates banks could see 18% higher pretax income once AI is fully embedded. They just disagree on how to get there.

Goldman Sachs CIO Marco Argenti has publicly rejected tracking AI at the individual level, comparing it to watching only one player on a football field. Goldman measures team-level results instead — how fast ideas go from concept to production, how quickly engineering backlogs shrink. He reports that employee sentiment has shifted from skepticism to genuine buy-in, and credits that to people discovering the tools on their own rather than feeling watched.

JPMorgan and Goldman are running what amounts to a live experiment on the biggest open question in AI workforce strategy: does pressure or autonomy produce better adoption? JPMorgan built the leaderboard. Goldman built the culture. Both are spending billions and both need their people to actually use the tools. Within a couple years the productivity data will make one of them look right and the other look very wrong. If your company recently handed you an AI tool and started asking how often you open it, this experiment isn't hypothetical for you. It's already your Monday morning.

ENTERPRISE

SAP Put $5.2 Billion Behind Open-Source AI Agents
Share X in

SAP just made an open-source startup the backbone of its AI agent strategy.

At its annual Sapphire conference, the German software giant announced a strategic investment in n8n, an open-source workflow automation platform, doubling the startup's valuation to $5.2 billion. SAP also signed a multiyear deal to embed n8n directly into Joule Studio, its AI agent builder. About 77% of global transaction revenue flows through SAP systems. If your company is one of them, you're about to get a visual way to build AI agent workflows inside the platform you already use.

n8n isn't a household name, but it's huge among developers:

+ 1.7 million monthly active builders and more than 1,400 enterprise customers, including Fortune 500 teams

+ Over 1,000 integrations with business tools, databases and AI models

+ Annual recurring revenue hit roughly $40 million in 2025, up over 450% year over year

n8n founder Jan Oberhauser announced the deal on stage at Sapphire in Orlando, framing the partnership around what enterprises actually need from agents: control, auditability, and trust.

That's what SAP is really paying for here — visibility. The persistent problem with AI agents in enterprise settings is that nobody can see what they're actually doing. An agent processes an invoice, routes a support ticket, triggers a procurement order, and the reasoning behind each step is invisible. n8n turns all of that into a visual diagram where every action is laid out and inspectable, so a compliance officer or auditor can understand exactly what happened and why. SAP CEO Christian Klein put it bluntly: for mission-critical processes, "almost right" just isn't good enough. And the early results back him up — Vanitha Ponnusamy at Sony said Joule Studio generated an end-to-end solution in 10 to 15 minutes, replacing what previously took three to four days.

SAP didn't build this in-house. This is a company that has spent more than 50 years constructing proprietary software and keeping customers firmly inside its ecosystem. Choosing an open-source project as the foundation for its AI agent infrastructure suggests SAP concluded that 1.7 million developers testing workflows in public is a better quality mechanism than anything it could build alone.

For enterprise customers, the model powering an AI agent is going to matter a lot less than being able to show exactly what that agent did. When a regulator asks why your AI made a specific decision, having a clear answer will matter more than having the most capable system. The governance layer is quietly becoming the product — and the fact that SAP outsourced it to an open-source community might be the most un-SAP thing the company has ever done.
