MARKETS

# $300 billion vanished from software stocks. Is this the SaaS-pocalypse?

Anthropic's latest AI upgrade erased a chunk of the enterprise software market in a single week.

On Monday, a wave of selling slammed software stocks after Anthropic [unveiled Claude Opus 4.6](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-is-a-space-to-think) and its "agent teams" feature, which lets users spin up AI agents that autonomously split up tasks and work on them in parallel. The timing was brutal. Investors, already jittery about whether traditional software companies could survive the agentic AI era, took one look at what these tools could do — financial analysis, document creation, agentic search, working across massive codebases — and panicked.

Salesforce fell to a 52-week low around $187. ServiceNow, Workday and other enterprise stalwarts [got dragged down with it](https://www.wsj.com/livecoverage/stock-market-today-dow-sp-500-nasdaq-02-03-2026). European software stocks [weren't spared either](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/ai-concerns-pummel-european-software-stocks-2026-02-03/), with SAP, Dassault Systèmes and others sliding as investors reassessed the entire sector. By midweek, roughly [$300 billion in market value had evaporated](https://www.forbes.com/sites/donmuir/2026/02/04/300-billion-evaporated-the-saaspocalypse-has-begun/) from software companies globally.

"The software companies were assumed to be winners from AI. But all of a sudden, you start to worry about whether you can earn the money back from your AI investments, and/or will you be outsmarted," Lars Skovgaard, senior investment strategist at Danske Bank, [told Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/ai-concerns-pummel-european-software-stocks-2026-02-03/).

The fear isn't abstract. When an AI agent team can autonomously handle the kind of work that once required a Salesforce license, a Workday subscription and a ServiceNow contract, the math starts to look uncomfortable for the companies selling those subscriptions. Anthropic's Scott White, head of product for enterprise, [told Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/anthropic-releases-ai-upgrade-market-punishes-software-stocks-2026-02-05/) that the company wants to "partner and actually lower the floor to get more value out of those tools" — a diplomatic way of saying that AI agents are coming for workflows that SaaS companies currently own.

This wasn't just Anthropic. OpenAI released GPT-5.3-Codex the same day, expanding its coding agent into a tool that can handle slide decks, spreadsheets, debugging and deployment.

The one-two punch felt less like a coincidence and more like a signal.

But not everyone is buying the doom narrative. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang [pushed back hard](https://www.wsj.com/livecoverage/stock-market-today-dow-sp-500-nasdaq-02-04-2026/card/nvidia-s-huang-says-it-s-illogical-to-think-ai-will-replace-software-tools-FqQnxoNLLQqwGXkEKM6z) during the selloff, calling the idea that AI would replace software tools "the most illogical thing in the world." His argument: AI doesn't eliminate the need for software — it creates more of it. More agents means more infrastructure, more orchestration layers, more tooling. Nvidia's stock [jumped 7%](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/06/nvidia-rises-7percent-as-ceo-says-660-billion-capex-buildout-is-sustainable.html) as he made the case that the $660 billion AI infrastructure buildout is sustainable.

The pressure on software valuations may have staying power. Baader Bank analyst Maximilien Pascaud [warned](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/ai-concerns-pummel-european-software-stocks-2026-02-03/) that "deflationary pressure on software-sector multiples could persist as long as the organic monetisation of AI is not clearly demonstrated."

**Enterprises are already voting with their budgets.** According to a [McKinsey interview](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/financial-services/our-insights/jpmorgan-chases-derek-waldron-on-building-an-ai-first-bank-culture) published this month, JPMorgan Chase — backed by an $18 billion annual technology budget — is "rewiring itself for a new era, harnessing agentic and gen AI to perform complex, multistep work." Derek Waldron, the bank's chief analytics officer, described the push as driven by a belief that "gen AI would be a highly democratizing technology." Translation: if JPMorgan can replace licensed software seats with AI agents running tasks internally, it will.

An [OpenAI enterprise report](https://openai.com/index/the-state-of-enterprise-ai-2025-report/) paints a similar picture. Organizations are moving past pilots and into production deployments, and the use cases that are sticking — document processing, code generation, workflow automation — overlap directly with what SaaS vendors sell today.

**Our Valley View:** The market's reaction was violent, but it wasn't irrational. AI agents that can autonomously handle financial analysis, document creation and cross-application workflows are a direct threat to the per-seat subscription model that built the modern software industry. Huang is right that AI creates demand for *new* software. But he's sidestepping the question that actually spooked investors: what happens to the *old* software? The SaaS companies that survive will be the ones that embed AI agents into their own products faster than those agents can replace them. The ones that can't will find themselves in a race they've already lost. Adapt or get vibe-coded out of existence, dear reader — that's the new law of enterprise software.

# AI's electricity bill is becoming yours

Your power bill probably went up last year — and AI data centers are a big reason why.

Household electricity rates [rose 13% nationally in 2025](https://links.message.bloomberg.com/s/c/SNr96Tqm7v1mIthO_HhIPMTiyygyU8l6FS6F-qwFhAhjAwzAVk1M0x6YjGwoxOpdt_Kc0UDGiWKTmI__muNt0_xfk3EZZZNXNAp9BVMINPjJpvxNEM7P0Q-hW3oORLhaiU5U5dG7aio1SS724ORKdQm5X3ihSv5rqrf6rimuq9YRB-FEquBwD2RJB8PXpuslZysK1GL8UaspVEz2arKE_Yp2qwLuarSLX5nGUuz6TcczIvLomnorjQ_QyiFxTQYP7dNWlsrqC3xu07hCYL3zvhoxQTQ74qEPhNoMgMVwNzg1JeRq0zgkz4gf3bRKihFyHc0TFnoRF3cxHlGHRNFarW-i3LODWuVynHtv3jbi5erz2UBa8kVpHcHo3ys/bZuxyPw_bGjJ4_t1iyLftMftd-zMZyzz/15), largely driven by data center development, according to a Bloomberg analysis. The servers humming behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini need a staggering amount of power, and the rest of us are increasingly helping foot the bill.

The scale is hard to overstate. A [Harvard-led study](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.09786v1) found that data centers already account for more than 4% of total US electricity consumption — projected to hit 6% by the end of this year. The IEA's first comprehensive AI energy report goes further still: by decade's end, US data centers will consume more electricity than aluminum, steel, cement and chemical production combined.

Let that sink in. The infrastructure powering your chatbot is on track to out-consume some of America's most energy-intensive industries.

The environmental footprint is equally grim. That same Harvard research found that [data centers' carbon intensity exceeds the US average by 48%](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09786), with 56% of their electricity still derived from fossil fuels. In 2023 alone, these facilities generated more than 105 million tons of CO₂ equivalent — roughly 2.18% of all US emissions. And that was before the current AI buildout really hit its stride.

**State governments are starting to push back.**

- New York lawmakers have [introduced legislation](https://www.engadget.com/big-tech/new-york-lawmakers-introduce-bill-that-aims-to-halt-data-center-development-for-three-years-224005266.html) to impose a three-year moratorium on large data center construction while the state assesses cumulative impacts on local grids, water supplies and communities.
- In Washington, legislators are [debating whether to force data centers to pay the full cost](https://www.axios.com/local/seattle/2026/02/03/washington-data-centers-legislature-electric-grid-cost-shift-ratepayers) of their electricity consumption rather than shifting the burden to residential ratepayers.
- A December state workgroup found that data centers are "expected to be the biggest drivers of increased electricity demand in the Pacific Northwest in the coming years," Axios reported.

The issue, at its core, is straightforward. When a data center moves into your region, it doesn't just plug in — it reshapes local energy economics entirely. Utilities that built infrastructure for predictable residential loads are suddenly serving facilities that can consume as much power as a small city. And when the grid needs upgrades to handle that, ratepayers — that's you, dear reader — get the bill.

The energy industry is scrambling to keep up:

- Pipeline company Williams Companies is [weighing acquisitions of gas-producing assets](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/williams-weighs-buying-gas-producing-assets-enhance-ai-energy-supply-2026-02-06/) specifically to feed the AI sector's appetite, Reuters reported.
- Real estate giant Prologis is [exploring new pools of investor capital](https://www.wsj.com/articles/prologis-might-fund-data-centers-with-new-pool-of-investor-capital-04b3769b) to fund data center development, per the Wall Street Journal.
- OpenAI — not typically known for lobbying on energy policy — has [filed comments](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/73ab270e-d753-4fde-8e32-eddc00d49ff1/ferc-anopr-reply-file-stamped.pdf) directly with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission.

When your business model depends on exponentially increasing compute, electricity isn't a line item. It's existential.

Some 20% of planned data center projects are already at risk of delays because the grid simply can't deliver power fast enough.

There are bright spots. Texas — a state whose independent grid has taken more than its fair share of abuse — has deployed [battery storage systems](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southcentral/2026/02/04/856861.htm) to smooth out supply volatility. "This new system, which allows it to use batteries more readily when needed, has improved grid flexibility and stability," Trudi Webster, a spokeswoman for the Electric Reliability Council of Texas, told Insurance Journal. But batteries are a band-aid when demand is growing this fast.

**Our Valley View**

AI companies are spending trillions on infrastructure while largely assuming power supply will keep pace. That bet is looking increasingly shaky. States from New York to Washington are moving to curb data center expansion or shift costs back onto the companies driving them, and residential ratepayers are waking up to the fact that their climbing bills are partially subsidizing Silicon Valley's ambitions. The AI firms that invest early in renewables, grid partnerships and genuine community buy-in will get to keep building. Everyone else may find that the real bottleneck for AI was never compute or chips — it was the plug in the wall.

ENTERPRISE

# Most enterprises are spending billions on AI to run in place

New research from McKinsey and BCG shows a widening gap between CEO confidence and what's actually changing inside companies.

A pair of major reports out this week from McKinsey and Boston Consulting Group paint a picture of an AI adoption landscape split in two: a small group of companies aggressively reshaping how they operate, and a much larger group buying tools, running pilots, and hoping for the best.

According to BCG's [2026 AI Radar survey](https://on.bcg.com/4pENtRu) of 640 CEOs, about 90% believe that by 2028, AI will redefine what success looks like in their industry. Nearly all of them expect AI agents to produce measurable returns this year. The follow-through hasn't matched.

[McKinsey's report](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/bold-accelerators-how-operations-leaders-are-pulling-ahead-using-ai) found that only about 21% of organizations have fundamentally redesigned even some of their workflows around generative AI. The rest are essentially bolting AI onto existing processes — the corporate equivalent of putting a Tesla engine in a horse-drawn carriage.

McKinsey calls the companies that have actually committed to overhauling operations "bold accelerators." These firms aren't just adopting AI tools. They're rethinking jobs, retraining teams, and rebuilding workflows from scratch. And the gap between them and everyone else is widening fast.

The disconnect isn't just about technology.

It's about organizational willpower. As [Accenture noted](https://hrexecutive.com/execs-ai-plans-fueled-by-fear-of-falling-behind-not-roi/), many organizations are "investing based on strategic necessity rather than proven returns" — a polite way of saying they're spending because they're terrified of falling behind, not because they know what they're doing.

That fear is showing up in the data. A separate [BCG and MIT Sloan Management Review study](https://web-assets.bcg.com/dc/c5/1bcbfdc0405c85fb14972a57c20a/the-emerging-agentic-enterprise-how-leaders-must-navigate-a-new-age-of-ai.pdf) on agentic AI found that 76% of executives now view AI agents as "more like a coworker than a tool." It's a revealing framing — and one that highlights how far ahead executive imagination has raced past organizational reality.

**AI providers are racing to close the gap.** On Wednesday, OpenAI [unveiled](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/openai-unveils-ai-agent-service-part-push-attract-businesses-2026-02-05/) a new agent service aimed at businesses, with CEO of applications Fidji Simo calling it "an intelligence layer that's going to help every enterprise turn on agents in a much easier way." Snowflake's recent [deal with OpenAI](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/02/what-snowflakes-deal-with-openai-tells-us-about-the-enterprise-ai-race/) further underscores how aggressively AI companies are courting enterprise customers who haven't yet figured out how to deploy the stuff they already bought.

The bottleneck isn't the AI itself. "How do we make AI reliable, scalable, auditable, and economically defensible? The surprising answer is not in the AI technology, nor in the cloud stack, nor in another round of system upgrades. It starts with data," Mark Dangelo, founder of AXTent, wrote in Thomson Reuters ([Thomsonreuters](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/technology/understanding-data-core-enterprise-acceleration/)). Most companies are sitting on decades of messy, siloed information that AI models can't actually use — what the industry calls "legacy data debt."

The sectors that *are* seeing real traction tend to be the ones where the work is already structured and rule-based. In finance and accounting, AI agents are already [managing 25% to 75% of tasks](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/ai-agents-managing-25-75-of-finance-and-accounting-tasks-for-many-cfos) for many CFOs, according to a Maximor report. But even there, the message from finance leaders is clear: "Human oversight is not resistance. It is responsible adoption. CFOs want automation that knows when to act and when to pause for judgment."

The legal industry is experiencing a similar tension. A [Thomson Reuters report](https://legal.thomsonreuters.com/blog/the-legal-market-at-a-crossroads-5-forces-reshaping-law-firm-success-in-2026/) found law firms at a crossroads, with AI adoption accelerating but firms struggling to integrate the technology into billable workflows that have remained essentially unchanged for decades.

There's a people problem, too. [Axios reported](https://www.axios.com/top) a growing gap between CEO enthusiasm and employee buy-in, with workers increasingly skeptical of leadership's AI messaging. When your boss says AI will "transform the business" but your day-to-day hasn't changed in 18 months, that skepticism is earned.

**Our Valley View:** The AI adoption gap is the defining enterprise story of 2026, and it's only going to get more uncomfortable. Companies that treat AI as a procurement exercise — buy the tool, run a pilot, put out a press release — will watch McKinsey's "bold accelerators" eat their lunch. The hard part was never the technology. It's convincing an organization of 10,000 people to actually change how they work. CEOs can believe whatever they want about 2028. The companies that will get there are the ones doing the painful, unglamorous work of redesigning jobs and cleaning up data *right now*. Everyone else is just running expensive pilots.
