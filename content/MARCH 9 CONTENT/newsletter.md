WORKFORCE

# Goldman can't find AI's productivity boost

Companies are spending hundreds of billions on AI. The economy hasn't noticed.

Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok said it plainly in a [recent analysis](https://www.apolloacademy.com/waiting-for-the-ai-j-curve/): "AI is everywhere except in the incoming macroeconomic data." By one estimate, AI accounted for just 0.2% of the nation's 2.2% GDP growth last year, [according to Apricitas Economics](https://www.apricitas.io/p/taiwans-modern-miracle).

Now there's a massive data set to back that up. The [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836) published a working paper surveying nearly 6,000 CFOs, CEOs and senior executives across the U.S., U.K., Germany and Australia — the first time central banks and research institutions asked [identical AI-focused questions](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34836/w34836.pdf) simultaneously.

The headline finding: **over 80% of firms report no impact from AI on either employment or productivity over the past three years.** Roughly 70% of those same firms are actively using AI. They just can't point to what it's doing for them.

Goldman senior economist Ronnie Walker coined a term for this gap: ["AI-nxiety"](https://fortune.com/2026/03/03/goldman-earnings-ai-anxiety-no-meaningful-impact-productivity-economy-30-percent-in-2-areas/) — corporate boardroom talk about AI completely untethered from measurable economic outcomes. Even the executives leading AI strategy use it just 1.5 hours per week on average; [one in four don't use it at all](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34836/w34836.pdf).

**The money keeps flowing anyway.** Goldman Sachs Research estimates AI companies will [invest more than $500 billion](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/why-ai-companies-may-invest-more-than-500-billion-in-2026) in infrastructure this year. Analyst Ryan Hammond flagged the risk: capital expenditures "can't grow forever without something to show for it," and a slowdown [poses a direct threat to valuations](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/why-ai-companies-may-invest-more-than-500-billion-in-2026). Some are already pulling back — [Gartner predicts](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-10-gartner-predicts-50-percent-of-organizations-will-abandon-plans-to-reduce-customer-service-workforce-due-to-ai) half of organizations will abandon plans to cut customer service staff through AI by 2027 as early implementations fall short.

Stanford economist Erik Brynjolfsson sees the glass half full. He argues we're in a "Productivity J-Curve" — measured productivity dips before it surges, because companies spend years reorganizing before the payoff clicks. He points to Q4 2025 GDP growth of 3.7% despite soft job numbers as a sign the curve may be bending upward.

But Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, raised a sharper question at a [recent event](https://ari.us/virtual-panel-ai-boom-or-bubble-economists-weigh-in/): "We're not creating any jobs now, and there's no AI productivity gains. What happens when we get some productivity gains here? Doesn't that mean job loss?"

The NBER data offers a quiet answer. [Two-thirds of expected job reductions](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34836/w34836.pdf) from AI are projected to come not from layoffs, but from companies simply hiring fewer people. Those are jobs that never get posted. Harder to miss, and harder to fight.

**OUR VALLEY VIEW**

The AI industry has been operating on a promise: spend now, reap later. But "later" keeps getting pushed back, and the checks keep getting bigger. Nearly everyone is using AI and almost no one can prove it's working at scale — not a death knell (the internet looked like a bad bet in 1999, too), but it means the biggest AI wagers are faith-based investments running on an increasingly impatient clock. The question isn't whether AI will eventually transform productivity. It's whether the money runs out before it does.

# Nvidia says it's done investing in AI labs

The company that powers nearly every AI model just said it's finished funding the companies that build them.

At the Morgan Stanley TMT Conference on Wednesday, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said the company's $30 billion investment in OpenAI ["might be the last"](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4878474-nvidia-corporation-nvda-presents-at-morgan-stanley-technology-media-and-telecom-conference) direct equity stake, and called its $10 billion Anthropic commitment ["probably"](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4878474-nvidia-corporation-nvda-presents-at-morgan-stanley-technology-media-and-telecom-conference) the final investment there, too. His reasoning: both companies are headed toward IPOs, and Nvidia doesn't see itself as a pre-IPO investor once they go public.

That explanation doesn't quite hold up. Strategic investors regularly participate in rounds right up to a company's public debut. As [TechCrunch noted](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/04/jensen-huang-says-nvidia-is-pulling-back-from-openai-and-anthropic-but-his-explanation-raises-more-questions-than-it-answers/), the rationale "raises more questions than it answers." There's also a tone shift: In January, Huang called OpenAI ["one of the most consequential companies of our time."](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/private-equity/nvidia-to-join-openais-current-funding-round-huang-says) Two months later, he's winding things down.

Huang dismissed any tension with the labs. But the less comfortable explanations have nothing to do with personal relationships. Nvidia controls roughly 90% to 95% of the market for advanced AI chips. When it pours billions into the same companies buying those chips, the arrangement looks circular. Michael Cusumano, a professor at MIT Sloan, put it [bluntly](https://www.cxodigitalpulse.com/nvidia-signals-end-to-investments-in-openai-and-anthropic-as-ipo-expectations-grow/): "Nvidia is investing $100 billion in OpenAI stock, and OpenAI is saying they are going to buy $100 billion or more of Nvidia chips... kind of a wash." Beyond optics, the cross-ownership [creates incentives](https://www.freedom969.com/politics/nvidias-100-billion-openai-play-raises-big-antitrust-issues) for Nvidia to favor its investment partners over competitors — a real antitrust concern.

This isn't hypothetical. Nvidia structured its [$20 billion acquisition of chip startup Groq](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bET4XJ5TcwE) last December specifically to [sidestep regulatory review](https://intuitionlabs.ai/pdfs/nvidia-s-20b-groq-deal-strategy-lpu-tech-antitrust.pdf). The company is clearly aware of where the lines are.

Nvidia isn't done spending — just spending differently. In January, it [announced](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-and-Lilly-Announce-Co-Innovation-AI-Lab-to-Reinvent-Drug-Discovery-in-the-Age-of-AI/default.aspx) a $1 billion co-innovation lab with Eli Lilly for AI-powered drug discovery — the kind of partnership that puts Nvidia's chips to work without the entanglement of owning a stake in a lab that buys your products. Nvidia's future isn't in picking which lab wins. It's in selling infrastructure to every company that wants to build.

**OUR VALLEY VIEW**

Nvidia spent the last two years playing both sides of the AI boom, selling the picks and shovels while buying stakes in the gold mines. That was always going to be temporary. Whether the pullback is driven by antitrust risk, IPO logistics, or simply recognizing that you shouldn't own your best customers, the signal is the same: the era of chipmakers bankrolling AI labs is over. What comes next will tell us whether this industry is maturing into something sustainable or still running on circular billions.

# Two engineers built OpenAI's most-used internal tool

Even the company building the world's most advanced AI couldn't find its own data.

OpenAI has 600 petabytes of data and 70,000 datasets. For a while, the people building the future of AI were stuck doing what everyone else does: hunting through similar-looking tables trying to figure out which one actually had what they needed.

"We have a lot of tables that are fairly similar, and I spend tons of time trying to figure out how they're different and which to use," one internal user [said](https://openai.com/index/inside-our-in-house-data-agent/). "Some include logged-out users, some don't."

Two engineers fixed it. They built an internal data agent — an AI that lets anyone at OpenAI ask questions about company data in plain English — and it quietly became the most widely used tool at the company, now serving [more than 3,500 users](https://openai.com/index/inside-our-in-house-data-agent/) across engineering, product, and research.

The agent was built using the same tools OpenAI sells to outside developers — GPT-5.2, Codex, and the Embeddings API — with roughly [70% of its code AI-generated](https://venturebeat.com/orchestration/openais-ai-data-agent-built-by-two-engineers-now-serves-4-000-employees-and). One of its best features is a memory layer that learns which tables to use over time. Without it, a routine query about ChatGPT Image Gen's logged-in daily active users took over 22 minutes. With memory enabled, the same question [took 82 seconds](https://openai.com/index/inside-our-in-house-data-agent/).

OpenAI made a point of noting that none of this required secret internal technology, describing the tools as ["the same tools we make available to developers everywhere."](https://openai.com/index/inside-our-in-house-data-agent/)

Every large company is chasing some version of this — AI that sits on top of messy, sprawling data and makes it useful without routing every question through an analytics team. Snowflake, Chevron, and others are already building [agent-ready data infrastructure](https://www.thoughtspot.com/press-releases/thoughtspot-unveils-dataspot-to-accelerate-agentic-analytics-for-every-databricks-customer), but the prerequisite is the same: data that's unified and governed enough for agents to actually work with.

The catch is trust. A [Sonar survey](https://www.sonarsource.com/company/press-releases/sonar-data-reveals-critical-verification-gap-in-ai-coding/) of more than 1,100 developers found that 96% don't fully trust AI-generated code, yet only 48% always review it before shipping. "Value is no longer defined by the speed of writing code, but by the confidence in deploying it," Sonar CEO Tariq Shaukat [said](https://www.sonarsource.com/company/press-releases/sonar-data-reveals-critical-verification-gap-in-ai-coding/). OpenAI's internal answer is an automated testing pipeline that catches errors before they reach users. Whether most organizations can replicate that discipline is another question entirely.

**OUR VALLEY VIEW**

The most revealing detail here isn't the agent itself. It's that OpenAI had the same "which table has my numbers?" problem as everyone else, and two engineers with publicly available tools fixed it. That's encouraging if you're trying to build something similar, and a little uncomfortable if you sell data analytics software for a living. The barrier to building useful AI agents is dropping fast. The question is whether your data is clean enough to meet it.
