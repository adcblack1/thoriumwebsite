Good Morning Thorium Valley. OpenAI loses somewhere between two and three dollars for every one it earns. Naturally, it wants to go public. HSBC projects the funding hole hits $200 billion by 2030, which is a lot of faith to ask from Wall Street — but when has that ever stopped anyone.

OpenAI burns $3 for every $1 of revenue
Share X in

OpenAI loses money on every dollar it earns. Now it wants to go public.

The company is projecting roughly $10 billion a year in revenue, the fastest ramp in tech history. It's also nowhere near enough to cover costs. For every dollar of revenue, OpenAI spends somewhere between $1.85 and $3.02, depending on the estimate. Either way, the more it sells, the more it loses — a "margin structure inversion" where chip and cloud providers capture more value than OpenAI itself.

Traditional SaaS companies run gross margins of 70–85%. OpenAI sits at about 41%, squeezed by an inference bill projected to hit $7.3 billion next year. Its seven-year Oracle cloud contract alone commits roughly $14 billion — more than its current annual revenue.

Until now, these numbers lived in leaked decks and confidential filings. An IPO means GAAP financials, quarterly calls, and analysts modeling a business that spends about $1.60 to make a dollar at the operating line.

OpenAI's internal projections lay out the bull case:

+ Revenue to $137 billion by 2030, roughly 40% above earlier forecasts, driven by ChatGPT subscriptions and a coming wave of AI agents.

+ Advertising as a real business. The ChatGPT ad pilot is generating $100 million in annualized run-rate revenue, with internal targets of $10 billion by 2030.

+ Compute getting cheaper. The whole model assumes inference costs fall faster than usage grows.

HSBC isn't buying it. The bank projects OpenAI's free cash flow stays negative through 2030, leaving a cumulative funding hole of more than $200 billion.

Anthropic, which just closed a $5.5 billion round at a valuation near $50 billion, has the same problem at smaller scale. PitchBook recently described the valuation race between the two labs less as a sign of strength than a measure of how much capital each needs to keep buying compute — and flagged that companies on a healthy IPO track don't usually need to issue press statements about strategic alignment.

That's the backdrop Bank of America's Michael Hartnett was looking at when he warned that the concentration of SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic IPOs draws comparisons to the speculative listing frenzy of the late 1920s. He wasn't calling a crash — he was warning that capital flowing to narrative over near-term profitability could slow AI's real-world deployment.

OpenAI has always sold today's losses as tomorrow's moat. Capture the users now, build the agents next, sell the ads after that, and the math eventually catches up. The IPO is where that pitch stops being told in private decks and starts being priced in public every weekday at 9:30 a.m. If Wall Street decides the $3-for-$1 math is a phase, OpenAI gets the cheapest capital in tech history. If it decides those numbers are the business, this becomes the most expensive lesson in the bubble cycle since 1999.

WORKFORCE

CEOs are publishing the layoff playbook
Share X in

For a while, the AI layoff story was something companies tried not to say out loud. That part is over.

In the past few weeks, several CEOs have published versions of essentially the same memo: AI is now good enough to do real work, the company is going to be smaller and flatter because of it, and the people who stay will get paid more to run AI instead of getting managed. And they're not being quiet about it — they're putting it in op-eds, SEC filings, and tweets.

+ Cloudflare's Matthew Prince wrote a WSJ op-ed arguing that AI systems can now evaluate workers with more precision than even the best managers — which means a lot of managers are doing a job a model already does.

+ Intuit's Sasan Goodarzi cut roughly 17% of the workforce in a memo filed with the SEC, saying AI had eliminated the coordination-heavy roles that used to hold the org chart together.

+ ClickUp's Zeb Evans cut a fifth of his staff, deployed 3,000 internal AI agents, and introduced cash salary bands of up to $1 million a year for employees who could demonstrate "100x impact" by building or managing AI systems.

+ Block's Jack Dorsey beat them all to it, cutting nearly half of the company's 10,000-person workforce in February and telling employees AI had made many of those roles unnecessary.

This isn't a handful of outliers. Mercer's Global Talent Trends report found that 99% of CEOs expect AI-driven layoffs in the next two years.

There is a counter-case worth holding onto. A Meta Superintelligence Labs paper published this month showed that a tiny replay script — one that blindly executes recorded actions without ever looking at the screen — outperforms frontier AI models on the benchmarks used to measure how agentic these systems really are. Some of the metrics CEOs are pointing to may be flattering the technology more than it deserves. Gartner is also still projecting that half of the companies cutting customer service jobs because of AI will end up rehiring for similar roles by 2027.

The memo is becoming a genre. Prince wrote the op-ed version, Goodarzi did the SEC version, Evans did the X version, and the next CEO who wants to cut headcount now has three pre-approved templates to choose from. That is the actual story. The "is it really AI?" debate is already being replaced by a shared executive vocabulary for explaining a smaller workforce as strategy instead of cost-cutting. By the time Gartner's 2027 rehire forecast comes due, the playbook will be old enough to vote.

STARTUPS

DeepSeek finally takes money it spent three years saying it didn't need
Share X in

For nearly three years, DeepSeek founder Liang Wenfeng insisted he didn't need outside investors. The Chinese AI lab was bankrolled by High-Flyer, the quant hedge fund Liang also runs — no VC roadmap, no quarterly pressure, just GPUs and researchers doing what they wanted. He built from one GPU to 10,000 without a single outside check.

That changed this week. DeepSeek closed its first outside round, a $300 million raise. The more interesting question is why a founder who built his identity around self-sufficiency finally changed his mind.

The ideology was as unusual as the funding model. Liang released models for free, slashed pricing below U.S. labs, and refused to play the user-acquisition game. "We didn't intend to be a disruptor; it just happened by accident," he said. He even argued that closed-source moats don't last — not even OpenAI's — so DeepSeek anchored its value in its team instead.

So what changed? Two things at once:

+ The gap closed. According to the 2026 Stanford AI Index, the leading American model is now just 2.7% ahead of its top Chinese competitor. DeepSeek is a big reason for that.

+ Staying ahead costs more than catching up. Training and serving frontier models at this scale costs more than even a successful hedge fund can quietly cover. Liang's old line that money was never the problem worked fine when the goal was catching up. It works less well when the goal is staying out front.

DeepSeek insists nothing changes — models stay open, pricing stays aggressive. But there's a real difference between a founder who answers to no one and a founder who just took $300 million from outside investors, no matter how aligned everyone says they are right now.

Liang spent three years turning DeepSeek into a counter-example to almost every assumption U.S. labs operate under: open source, no VC, no growth-at-all-costs pressure, no need to win on user numbers. The funding round doesn't kill that pitch overnight, but it puts it on a clock. Investors are patient until they aren't, and "AGI belongs to everyone" is a much easier thing to say when the only name on the cap table is a hedge fund you also run. The real test isn't whether DeepSeek keeps shipping competitive models. It's whether the philosophy survives its first actual board meeting.
