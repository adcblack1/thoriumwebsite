JPMorgan Built an AI Scoreboard for Its Engineers
Share X in

JPMorgan Chase is keeping score on which of its engineers use AI.

The bank built internal dashboards that track individual usage of AI coding tools across the company, and they're already live. Of nearly 70,000 employees given access to GitHub Copilot, only about 24,000 show up as active. Global CIO Lori Beer said the productivity gains were significant enough that the internal conversation shifted from whether engineers should use AI to why they aren't all using it.

The numbers back that up. JPMorgan's AI tools have boosted engineer productivity by 10% to 20%. CEO Jamie Dimon told Bloomberg this week that the bank will be "hiring more AI people and probably less bankers in certain categories," calling current AI use "the tip of the iceberg." The company's tech budget is approaching $20 billion this year, up roughly $2 billion from 2025.

Every major bank on Wall Street is pushing AI adoption hard right now — Citigroup made training mandatory for 240,000 employees, Bank of America says AI now handles 30 billion client interactions, and Morgan Stanley estimates banks could see 18% higher pretax income once AI is fully embedded. They just disagree on how to get there.

Goldman Sachs CIO Marco Argenti has publicly rejected tracking AI at the individual level, comparing it to watching only one player on a football field. Goldman measures team-level results instead — how fast ideas go from concept to production, how quickly engineering backlogs shrink. He reports that employee sentiment has shifted from skepticism to genuine buy-in, and credits that to people discovering the tools on their own rather than feeling watched.

JPMorgan and Goldman are running what amounts to a live experiment on the biggest open question in AI workforce strategy: does pressure or autonomy produce better adoption? JPMorgan built the leaderboard. Goldman built the culture. Both are spending billions and both need their people to actually use the tools. Within a couple years the productivity data will make one of them look right and the other look very wrong. If your company recently handed you an AI tool and started asking how often you open it, this experiment isn't hypothetical for you. It's already your Monday morning.
