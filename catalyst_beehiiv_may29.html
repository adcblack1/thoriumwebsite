ENTERPRISE

Manulife's AI Approves Life Insurance in Two Minutes
Share X in

Manulife used to take days to process a life insurance application. An AI engine called MAUDE now does it in two minutes.

MAUDE handles 58% of eligible cases in Canada on its own, tailoring the application based on how people answer earlier questions, which lets it cut up to 40% of medical questions from the process entirely. By December, automatic approvals had jumped 56% from pre-launch levels.

That's one tool in one country. Manulife, one of the largest insurers in the world, has been rolling AI into practically everything. The company deployed GenAI tools to all 40,000-plus employees, and 75% are actually using them — a high engagement rate for any enterprise rollout. The results show up across the business:

+ Developer productivity jumped 30% from AI coding assistants

+ U.S. retirement sales generated roughly 2,000 AI-driven leads and about 390 closed deals, with advisors spending 80% less time prepping for meetings

+ Evident's AI Index ranked Manulife first in life insurance AI maturity across 30 major insurers

Rather than plugging a general-purpose chatbot into everything, Manulife brought in outside partners to run AI agents in production and fine-tune specialized models through reinforcement learning — training AI on narrow tasks through trial and error rather than asking one giant model to do everything. The company expects the whole push to generate more than $1 billion in enterprise value by 2027.

But every one of those numbers measures speed. How fast cases get approved. How much prep time advisors save. How quickly code ships. Not a single published metric measures whether the AI is making the right decisions. In an industry where the product is literally a promise to pay when something goes wrong, that matters. The only large-scale independent data on AI-automated insurance decisions comes from lawsuits alleging that denial rates at UnitedHealthcare rose sharply during its AI rollout. Manulife is approving applications, not denying claims, so the risk profile is different — but the underlying question is the same.

Not every insurer is drawing the same conclusions, either. Acrisure, a major brokerage, cut 2,250 employees this month citing advances in AI. Manulife is automating and investing in AI talent. Same technology, very different bets on what it means for people.

Manulife has probably the clearest public blueprint for multi-function AI deployment in financial services right now, and it is genuinely worth studying. But the measurement problem should bother you. We know exactly how fast the machine runs. We have no published evidence that it's accurate. That's not unique to Manulife but rather the default across every industry deploying AI at scale, where velocity is the metric and correctness is assumed. Eventually, someone's going to check.

WORKFORCE

C3.ai's comeback plan has zero traditional engineers
Share X in

Tom Siebel, the billionaire who built Siebel Systems into a company Oracle acquired for $5.85 billion, just returned as CEO of C3.ai after stepping aside for health reasons — and immediately called his own company's performance "candidly unacceptable." Five days back in the chair, he laid out a turnaround plan on CNBC that made a lot of people in software engineering uncomfortable: C3.ai plans to use AI tools like Anthropic's Claude and Cursor to handle engineering work. The traditional software engineer role, he said, is going away at his company.

The financial picture explains the urgency. C3.ai's preliminary fiscal year results tell a brutal story:

+ ~$265 million in revenue against roughly $480 million in GAAP operating losses — burning about $2 for every $1 earned

+ ~$770 million in cash and investments remaining

+ A restructuring plan expected to deliver $130–$140 million in annualized savings, mostly from headcount cuts

There's a confession buried in all of this that's easy to miss. C3.ai sells AI to the world's biggest organizations — Shell, the U.S. Department of Defense — but it had hundreds of engineers building those products the old-fashioned way, and it was hemorrhaging money doing it. Siebel's implicit argument is that the reason his AI company was failing is that it wasn't actually using AI itself.

What C3.ai is actually hiring for right now, though, doesn't quite match the rhetoric. The company's careers page still lists traditional software engineering roles. One posting for an "AI-First Release Manager" tells you the reality is probably less about firing every coder and more about transforming what the job looks like — less writing code from scratch, more directing AI tools and reviewing what they produce.

Not everyone buys the framing. Julie Geller, a principal research director at Info-Tech Research Group, argued that C3.ai's SEC filings suggest these are simply traditional cost cuts driven by financial pressure — not AI-driven transformation. It's an irony she's quick to point out: a company selling AI efficiency going through a restructuring that looks a lot like old-fashioned belt-tightening.

Siebel says the impact will become visible starting around Q3 and over the following few years.

Most of the "AI is replacing engineers" conversation has been theoretical or happening inside private companies where nobody has to show their work. Siebel's version comes with a quarterly report card, since C3.ai is publicly traded and he gave himself a deadline. If the product quality holds and revenue starts growing with a smaller engineering org, every engineering leader with a budget is going to have a conversation they weren't planning on. If it doesn't, we get the most expensive public proof yet that "just use Claude" isn't a workforce strategy. We rarely get to watch one of these bets play out this transparently.

STARTUPS

Harvey's $11 Billion Case Against the Billable Hour
Share X in

Winston Weinberg lasted one year as a junior associate at O'Melveny & Myers. Three and a half years later, the legal AI company he built after quitting is valued at $11 billion with more than $1 billion in total funding raised.

In a Financial Times interview, Weinberg broke down a pricing problem anyone who's ever gotten a professional services bill will recognize. If you pay a law firm $100,000 for a deal, you're really paying for a partner's few hours of judgment at $3,000 an hour — but the bill shows up as dozens of junior associate hours doing research at $1,000 each. His prediction: law firms will start offering pricing menus. Fixed fees for the tasks AI handles. Premium rates for judgment calls that require a human with 20 years of experience. The billable hour won't disappear, but its monopoly is ending.

The strange part is that for the firms charging the most, the math is working fine right now. Top partner rates have hit $3,400 an hour and overall rates climbed 7.4% in 2025, roughly double inflation. Clients keep paying because the advice they actually care about is genuinely worth it. All the pressure is on the layers underneath — the due diligence, the contract review, the work that used to take a first-year two weeks and now takes an AI a few hours. As Thomson Reuters put it in its 2026 State of the US Legal Market report, firms are deploying technology that finishes in minutes what once took hours, then trying to bill for it by the hour.

That's where Harvey lives, and the company's scale is hard to ignore:

+ 142,000 lawyers across 1,500 organizations in 60 countries use the platform

+ More than 65 of the AmLaw 100, the biggest law firms in the country, are clients

+ Over 25,000 custom AI agents handle M&A, due diligence, contract drafting and document review

Weinberg told the FT that AI will also force firms to rethink how they train juniors. Juniors still need to do due diligence 10 or 20 times to learn the craft, but not 1,000 times. And he was clear this goes well beyond law: "This will happen in banking. This will happen in all the professional services."

Not everyone is waiting for Big Law to evolve on its own terms. Moritz, an AI-native law firm out of Y Combinator, raised $9 million to build a practice from scratch with no billable hour model to protect. Harvey sells AI to existing firms. Moritz is trying to be the firm — and that's probably the more interesting test of whether incumbents can adapt fast enough.

One thing worth noting: Harvey shares adoption metrics freely but no public revenue figures. At $11 billion with over $1 billion raised, Sequoia co-leading three consecutive rounds signals serious investor conviction. But the gap between what we know about their reach and what we can verify about their business is worth watching.

The most telling part of Weinberg's interview is the throwaway line about banking, consulting, and professional services being next. Any industry where clients pay by the hour for a mix of judgment and routine work is staring at the same unbundling. The firms that figure out how to price the human part separately from the automated part will probably come out ahead. The ones that keep billing AI-speed work at human-speed rates are going to get a very direct question from their clients, and probably sooner than they think.
