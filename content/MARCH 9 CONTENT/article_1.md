# Goldman can't find AI's productivity boost

Companies are spending hundreds of billions on AI. The economy hasn't noticed.

Apollo Global Management chief economist Torsten Slok said it plainly in a [recent analysis](https://www.apolloacademy.com/waiting-for-the-ai-j-curve/): "AI is everywhere except in the incoming macroeconomic data." By economic analyst Joseph Politano's calculation, AI's contribution to U.S. economic growth last year was essentially zero, accounting for just 0.2% of the nation's 2.2% GDP growth, [according to Apricitas Economics](https://www.apricitas.io/p/taiwans-modern-miracle).

Now there's a massive data set to back that up. The [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836), a leading nonpartisan economics organization, published a working paper surveying nearly 6,000 CFOs, CEOs and senior executives across the U.S., U.K., Germany and Australia. The surveys were conducted in coordination with the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Bank of England, Deutsche Bundesbank and Macquarie University — making this the first time these institutions asked [identical AI-focused questions](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34836/w34836.pdf) simultaneously.

The headline finding: **over 80% of firms report no impact from AI on either employment or productivity over the past three years.** More than 90% specifically report no change in employment, and 89% report no change in labor productivity.

Roughly 70% of those same firms are actively using AI. They're deploying it for text generation, data processing, customer-facing chatbots. They just can't point to what it's doing for them.

Goldman senior economist Ronnie Walker coined a term for this gap: ["AI-nxiety"](https://fortune.com/2026/03/03/goldman-earnings-ai-anxiety-no-meaningful-impact-productivity-economy-30-percent-in-2-areas/) — where corporate boardroom talk about AI has become completely untethered from measurable economic outcomes.

Even the executives leading AI strategy don't seem all that engaged with the tools themselves. The [NBER study found](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34836/w34836.pdf) that top leaders use AI an average of just 1.5 hours per week. One in four don't use it at all.

**The money keeps flowing anyway.** Goldman Sachs Research estimates that AI companies will [invest more than $500 billion](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/why-ai-companies-may-invest-more-than-500-billion-in-2026) in AI infrastructure this year, spanning data centers, chips and power generation. That's on top of record-setting private funding rounds, including OpenAI's recent $110 billion raise.

Ryan Hammond, a Goldman Sachs Research analyst, flagged what all that spending means if the returns don't show up. "The timing of an eventual slowdown in capex growth poses a risk to these companies' valuations," Hammond [wrote](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/why-ai-companies-may-invest-more-than-500-billion-in-2026). Capital expenditures — the big-ticket infrastructure spending that AI companies have been ramping up — can't grow forever without something to show for it.

Some organizations are already pulling back. [Gartner predicts](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-10-gartner-predicts-50-percent-of-organizations-will-abandon-plans-to-reduce-customer-service-workforce-due-to-ai) that 50% of organizations will abandon plans to cut customer service staff through AI by 2027, as early implementations fall short of expectations.

Stanford economist Erik Brynjolfsson sees the glass half full. He argues we're in what he calls the "Productivity J-Curve" — when a transformative technology arrives, measured productivity dips before it surges, because companies spend years reorganizing and experimenting before the payoff clicks. He points to fourth-quarter 2025 GDP growth of 3.7% despite soft job numbers as a sign the curve may be starting to bend upward.

But Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, raised a sharper question at a [recent event](https://ari.us/virtual-panel-ai-boom-or-bubble-economists-weigh-in/) with economists from Goldman Sachs and Yale: "We're not creating any jobs now, and there's no AI productivity gains. What happens when we get some productivity gains here? Doesn't that mean job loss?"

The NBER data offers a quiet answer. [Two-thirds of expected job reductions](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34836/w34836.pdf) from AI are projected to come not from layoffs, but from companies simply hiring fewer people. Those are jobs that never get posted. Harder to miss, and harder to fight.

**OUR VALLEY VIEW**

The AI industry has been operating on a promise: spend now, reap later. But "later" keeps getting pushed back, and the checks keep getting bigger. Nearly everyone is using AI and almost no one can prove it's working at scale — not a death knell (the internet looked like a bad bet in 1999, too), but it means the biggest AI wagers are faith-based investments running on an increasingly impatient clock. The question isn't whether AI will eventually transform productivity. It's whether the money runs out before it does.
