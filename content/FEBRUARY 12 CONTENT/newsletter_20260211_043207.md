WORKFORCE

# Companies are blaming AI for layoffs the AI isn't actually driving

January layoffs surged to their worst levels since 2009 — AI was cited for just 7% of the cuts.

U.S. employers [announced 108,435 job cuts](https://www.reuters.com/business/world-at-work/ups-amazon-boost-us-planned-layoffs-january-challenger-survey-shows-2026-02-05/) in January, according to outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas — a 40% jump from the same month last year. Hiring plans, meanwhile, collapsed to their lowest January level since the firm started tracking the data in 2005. The headline culprits: government belt-tightening, tariff uncertainty and, increasingly, AI.

AI was [cited as the key reason for around 7% of the cuts](https://www.businessinsider.com/january-layoffs-cuts-highest-level-since-2009-challenger-2026-2), according to Challenger's data. That's not nothing. But it's a far cry from the narrative that robots are coming for your desk.

"It's difficult to say how big an impact AI is having on layoffs specifically," Andy Challenger, a workplace expert at Challenger, Gray & Christmas, [told Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/world-at-work/ups-amazon-boost-us-planned-layoffs-january-challenger-survey-shows-2026-02-05/). "We know leaders are talking about AI, many companies want to implement it in operations, and the market appears to be reshaping."

The gap between the talking and the implementing is where this story gets interesting — and where it should concern you, whether you're a CEO, a middle manager, or someone refreshing LinkedIn a little too often.

**The narrative doesn't match the numbers.** More than 80% of enterprises [report no tangible impact on EBIT](https://www.abaka.ai/blog/enterprise-ai-fortune-500-pilots-to-production) from their generative AI initiatives. MIT's NANDA report — its State of AI in Business Report 2025 ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/peer-community/post/mits-nanda-report-state-ai-business-report-2025-finds-95-enterprise-ai-initiatives-have-delivered-zero-roi-buy-strategies)) — found that 95% of enterprise AI initiatives have delivered zero ROI. Not low ROI. Zero.

So if the AI isn't actually generating returns, what exactly is replacing those workers?

Andrew Stettner, senior director for economic security at the National Employment Law Project, [put it plainly to CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/layoffs-unemployment-jobs-economy-jobless-benefits/): "I don't think these companies are doing layoffs because they know AI can replace workers, but I think they're investing in it."

Read that again. Companies are cutting staff and investing in AI. Those are two separate decisions being packaged as one.

There's a well-worn playbook here. When Wall Street rewards "efficiency" and "AI transformation" narratives, executives have every incentive to frame routine cost-cutting as forward-thinking strategy. Announcing layoffs alongside an AI initiative makes the cuts sound visionary instead of desperate. It's not a conspiracy — it's just good investor relations.

The professional services industry offers a useful case study. Thomson Reuters' [2026 AI in Professional Services Report](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/reports/2026-ai-in-professional-services-report) found that AI adoption has indeed hit critical mass across legal, tax, and accounting firms. But deployment hasn't kept pace with the hype. One [anonymous U.S.-based chief legal officer](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/technology/ai-in-professional-services-report-2026/) admitted that firms "are reluctant — they claim it would compromise quality and fidelity. I think they are threatened by it."

Mike Abbott, head of the Thomson Reuters Institute, [called 2026](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/reports/2026-ai-in-professional-services-report) "the strategic phase of AI — one where organizations redefine workflows, reshape value, and build AI directly into the foundation of their business strategy."

That's a nice way of saying most firms are still figuring out the basics.

The academic research backs this up. A [comprehensive review of AI and employment](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15265v1) studies found that while AI-exposed occupations have actually seen employment *growth* in recent years, the displacement effects remain concentrated in narrow, task-specific areas — think data entry and basic customer service — rather than the wholesale white-collar replacement that dominates headlines.

None of this means AI won't eventually reshape the labor market in profound ways. A [working paper from the National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487) on the macroeconomics of AI suggests that over the next decade, the technology could meaningfully alter how firms allocate labor. But "over the next decade" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.

The immediate reality is messier. [According to Challenger](https://www.businessinsider.com/january-layoffs-cuts-highest-level-since-2009-challenger-2026-2), the actual drivers of January's cuts tell their own story:

- **Contract losses** drove the largest share — nearly 29%
- **Government spending pullbacks**, tariff jitters, and plain old restructuring accounted for much of the rest
- **AI**, at 7%, is a sideshow being billed as the main event

---

**Our Valley View:** The uncomfortable truth is that "AI layoffs" are mostly just layoffs. Companies are wielding the AI narrative like a Swiss Army knife — it impresses investors, justifies headcount reduction, and signals forward momentum all at once. But until that 95% zero-ROI figure starts moving in a meaningful direction, the gap between AI as boardroom talking point and AI as actual workforce replacement remains enormous. The workers losing their jobs deserve honesty about why, and investors deserve honesty about what they're actually funding. Right now, neither group is getting it.

BIG TECH

# Musk's $1.25 trillion space-AI megamerger is losing its architects

Two co-founders walked out of xAI in 48 hours — right as the company tries to pull off the largest merger in history.

On Monday, xAI co-founder Jimmy Ba [announced his departure](https://www.businessinsider.com/elon-musk-xai-loses-second-cofounder-jimmy-ba-2026-2) from the AI lab, just two days after fellow co-founder Tony Wu [resigned](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/10/elon-musk-xai-co-founder-tony-wu.html). The exits bring the total number of departed xAI co-founders to four out of the original 12 — a pattern that's starting to look less like natural turnover and more like a trend.

Wu, in a post announcing his departure, struck a diplomatic tone: "It's time for my next chapter. It is an era with full possibilities: a small team armed with AIs can move mountains and redefine what's possible." Ba was more cryptic, writing that it's "time to recalibrate my gradient on the big picture," adding that "2026 is gonna be insane and likely the busiest (and most consequential) year for the future of our species."

Recalibrating your gradient at a company that's [burning roughly $1 billion a month](https://fintool.com/news/xai-146-billion-quarterly-loss-cash-burn) is one way to put it.

The departures land in the middle of what the [Wall Street Journal reported](https://www.wsj.com/tech/elon-musk-xai-spacex-merger-2896ae1e) as Musk's plan to merge xAI with SpaceX in a deal that would create a combined entity valued at approximately $1.25 trillion — the largest corporate merger ever attempted. The logic, according to Musk: "The lowest-cost place to put AI will be space. That will be true within two years, maybe three."

That claim might sound like the kind of thing you'd hear from a Bond villain, but it's not entirely without basis. A [Google research paper](https://research.google.com/pubs/papers.html) published late last year explored the feasibility of space-based machine learning data centers — solar-powered satellites networked together to run AI workloads. The authors propose that "instead of transmitting power to Earth from space, we propose a future that includes space-based ML 'data centers' consisting of many solar-powered satellites networked via free-space optical" links. Their analysis suggests launch costs to low-Earth orbit could reach below $200 per kilogram by the mid-2030s, which would make the economics start to pencil out.

"Start to pencil out" and "ready for a trillion-dollar bet" are very different statements. xAI consumed [$7.8 billion in cash](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/musks-xai-burned-about-8-bln-cash-in-first-9-mths-of-2025--bloomberg-4438356) in the first nine months of 2025 alone. SpaceX, while profitable, would now be tethered to an AI company whose mission to ["understand the true nature of the universe"](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/10/elon-musk-xai-co-founder-tony-wu.html) has yet to translate into revenue anywhere near its costs. According to [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/two-co-founders-elon-musks-xai-resign-joining-exodus-2026-02-11/), the merger would need to survive regulatory scrutiny and FCC review of the orbital data center concept — a concept that many aerospace engineers view with deep skepticism.

All of this needs to happen on a timeline. Wall Street is [anticipating a breakout year for IPOs](https://www.reuters.com/business/mega-ipos-loom-wall-street-anticipates-breakout-year-listings-2026-02-09/), with a potential SpaceX-xAI listing among the most closely watched. Losing founding technical talent right before you're supposed to convince public market investors that your company has a coherent, executable vision is — to put it mildly — not ideal.

* The co-founder departures follow a broader pattern of senior exits across AI labs. Thinking Machines Lab, founded by former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati, recently lost three of its six original co-founders, with four of the five departing employees heading to OpenAI.
* xAI's Grok chatbot has faced its own challenges, with a Common Sense Media report recently finding that it is [not safe for teens](https://www.commonsensemedia.org).
* SpaceX and xAI were already in [talks to merge](https://www.wsj.com/tech/elon-musk-xai-spacex-merger-2896ae1e) ahead of a planned IPO, suggesting this deal has been in the works for months regardless of personnel changes.

![](image)

**Our Valley View**

The vision of orbital AI data centers powered by unlimited solar energy is genuinely compelling on a long enough time horizon. The Google research makes a real scientific case. But Musk isn't asking investors to bet on a 2035 timeline — he's asking them to bet on a combined entity right now, one that pairs the most valuable private company in the world with an AI lab hemorrhaging both cash and co-founders. If xAI can't retain the people who built it, the real question is whether anyone will be left to put it there.

HARDWARE

# AI's chip addiction is about to hit your wallet

The AI boom is driving up the price of your next phone, laptop, and gaming console — and the squeeze is only getting started.

For the first time in roughly 30 years, Nvidia will not release new consumer gaming GPUs this cycle. The company has [shelved its RTX 50 "Super" refresh entirely and pushed back the RTX 60-series](https://www.techpowerup.com/346010/no-nvidia-geforce-rtx-50-super-gpus-this-year-rtx-60-series-also-pushed-back), diverting production capacity to the AI data center chips that now dominate its revenue. A global memory chip shortage — driven by the same AI boom — is rippling across the consumer electronics supply chain, hitting smartphones, PCs, and gaming hardware.

The semiconductor industry is "a bit panicked," Zhao Haijun, co-chief executive of Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corp., [told the Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/tech/smic-ceo-says-industry-panicked-about-memory-supply-shortage-f0e1aaed). "The robust demand for memory chips by AI networks has squeezed the supply for other sectors, such as phones, particularly in the low-end market."

AI data centers need a special type of memory called HBM — high-bandwidth memory — to power the chips that train and run models like GPT-5 and Gemini. Memory manufacturers like Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron have been racing to meet that demand by converting production lines away from the standard memory chips that go into everyday devices. The result? Not enough chips for everyone else.

Qualcomm is already feeling the pain. The chipmaker's stock [sank roughly 10%](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/05/qualcomm-stock-memory-shortage-warning.html) after it issued a warning that the memory crunch would dampen smartphone sales for the next several quarters. CEO Cristiano Amon put it bluntly in a [call with analysts](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/qualcomm-forecasts-quarterly-results-below-estimates-memory-shortage-dampens-2026-02-04/): "I'm very happy with the business — I just wish we had more memory."

Amon explained that Chinese phone makers in particular are "bringing down their inventory levels to adjust to their memory supply," [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/qualcomm-forecasts-quarterly-results-below-estimates-memory-shortage-dampens-2026-02-04/). Arm, whose chip designs power the vast majority of the world's smartphones, [also missed revenue expectations](https://www.reuters.com/business/qualcomm-arm-bear-brunt-memory-shortage-smartphone-chip-sales-disappoint-2026-02-05/) for the same reason.

"As with other companies producing chips for devices, Qualcomm's expecting to be impacted by the worldwide memory crunch for the next several quarters, particularly via some of its Chinese customers," Bob O'Donnell, chief analyst at TECHnalysis Research, [told Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/qualcomm-forecasts-quarterly-results-below-estimates-memory-shortage-dampens-2026-02-04/).

The damage isn't limited to phones.

Gaming is getting squeezed from two directions — Nvidia pulling back on consumer GPUs *and* rising component costs. Valve's upcoming Steam Machine, a PC-console hybrid, may be the canary in the coal mine. [Ars Technica reported](https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2026/02/why-a-bump-to-700-could-be-a-death-sentence-for-the-steam-machine/) that a price bump to $700 could be a "death sentence" for the device, since Valve lacks the purchasing power of Sony or Microsoft.

"Large console makers like Sony and Microsoft can commit to tens of millions of orders, and have strong negotiating power," Daniel Ahmad, analyst at Niko Partners, [told Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2026/02/why-a-bump-to-700-could-be-a-death-sentence-for-the-steam-machine/). "The Steam Machine, on the other hand, is a niche product that cannot benefit in the same way when it comes to procurement."

Joost van Dreunen, founder of Superdata Research, [echoed this concern](https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2026/02/why-a-bump-to-700-could-be-a-death-sentence-for-the-steam-machine/), noting that "Sony and Microsoft likely will have locked in more favorable component pricing before the current spike." Smaller hardware makers don't have that luxury.

**The broader price picture is grim.** IDC projects PC prices could rise between 4% and 6% under a moderate scenario, with more pessimistic forecasts running even higher. Phones face similar headwinds. According to a [McKinsey report on the semiconductor industry](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/semiconductors/our-insights/hiding-in-plain-sight-the-underestimated-size-of-the-semiconductor-industry), the true scale of the chip market — and its downstream effects on consumer pricing — has been systematically underestimated. McKinsey [also flagged](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/semiconductors/our-insights/memory-are-challenges-ahead) that the memory segment specifically faces structural challenges as AI demand reshapes how and where production capacity gets allocated.

None of this is temporary. TSMC is [planning major upgrades to its Japan chip plant](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-05/tsmc-plans-major-upgrade-of-japan-chip-plant-in-win-for-takaichi), but new fab capacity takes years to come online. In the meantime, every wafer dedicated to HBM for Nvidia's AI chips is a wafer that isn't making the DRAM in your phone or the NAND in your laptop.

**Our Valley View**

For years, the AI industry's enormous appetite for compute was somebody else's problem — the hyperscalers writing billion-dollar checks, the utilities scrambling to keep the lights on. That era is over. When Nvidia stops making gaming GPUs and your next phone costs more because Samsung is too busy building memory for data centers, AI's cost is no longer abstract. It's sitting on the price tag at Best Buy. The uncomfortable truth is that the companies building AI have decided — implicitly, through their purchasing power — that training the next frontier model matters more than keeping your laptop affordable. Whether consumers agree is another question entirely.
