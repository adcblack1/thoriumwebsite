# AI's electricity bill is becoming yours

Your power bill probably went up last year — and AI data centers are a big reason why.

Household electricity rates [rose 13% nationally in 2025](https://links.message.bloomberg.com/s/c/SNr96Tqm7v1mIthO_HhIPMTiyygyU8l6FS6F-qwFhAhjAwzAVk1M0x6YjGwoxOpdt_Kc0UDGiWKTmI__muNt0_xfk3EZZZNXNAp9BVMINPjJpvxNEM7P0Q-hW3oORLhaiU5U5dG7aio1SS724ORKdQm5X3ihSv5rqrf6rimuq9YRB-FEquBwD2RJB8PXpuslZysK1GL8UaspVEz2arKE_Yp2qwLuarSLX5nGUuz6TcczIvLomnorjQ_QyiFxTQYP7dNWlsrqC3xu07hCYL3zvhoxQTQ74qEPhNoMgMVwNzg1JeRq0zgkz4gf3bRKihFyHc0TFnoRF3cxHlGHRNFarW-i3LODWuVynHtv3jbi5erz2UBa8kVpHcHo3ys/bZuxyPw_bGjJ4_t1iyLftMftd-zMZyzz/15), largely driven by data center development, according to a Bloomberg analysis. The servers humming behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini need a staggering amount of power, and the rest of us are increasingly helping foot the bill.

The scale is hard to overstate. A [Harvard-led study](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.09786v1) found that data centers already account for more than 4% of total US electricity consumption — projected to hit 6% by the end of this year. The IEA's first comprehensive AI energy report goes further still: by decade's end, US data centers will consume more electricity than aluminum, steel, cement and chemical production combined.

Let that sink in. The infrastructure powering your chatbot is on track to out-consume some of America's most energy-intensive industries.

The environmental footprint is equally grim. That same Harvard research found that [data centers' carbon intensity exceeds the US average by 48%](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09786), with 56% of their electricity still derived from fossil fuels. In 2023 alone, these facilities generated more than 105 million tons of CO₂ equivalent — roughly 2.18% of all US emissions. And that was before the current AI buildout really hit its stride.

**State governments are starting to push back.**

- New York lawmakers have [introduced legislation](https://www.engadget.com/big-tech/new-york-lawmakers-introduce-bill-that-aims-to-halt-data-center-development-for-three-years-224005266.html) to impose a three-year moratorium on large data center construction while the state assesses cumulative impacts on local grids, water supplies and communities.
- In Washington, legislators are [debating whether to force data centers to pay the full cost](https://www.axios.com/local/seattle/2026/02/03/washington-data-centers-legislature-electric-grid-cost-shift-ratepayers) of their electricity consumption rather than shifting the burden to residential ratepayers.
- A December state workgroup found that data centers are "expected to be the biggest drivers of increased electricity demand in the Pacific Northwest in the coming years," Axios reported.

The issue, at its core, is straightforward. When a data center moves into your region, it doesn't just plug in — it reshapes local energy economics entirely. Utilities that built infrastructure for predictable residential loads are suddenly serving facilities that can consume as much power as a small city. And when the grid needs upgrades to handle that, ratepayers — that's you, dear reader — get the bill.

The energy industry is scrambling to keep up:

- Pipeline company Williams Companies is [weighing acquisitions of gas-producing assets](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/williams-weighs-buying-gas-producing-assets-enhance-ai-energy-supply-2026-02-06/) specifically to feed the AI sector's appetite, Reuters reported.
- Real estate giant Prologis is [exploring new pools of investor capital](https://www.wsj.com/articles/prologis-might-fund-data-centers-with-new-pool-of-investor-capital-04b3769b) to fund data center development, per the Wall Street Journal.
- OpenAI — not typically known for lobbying on energy policy — has [filed comments](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/73ab270e-d753-4fde-8e32-eddc00d49ff1/ferc-anopr-reply-file-stamped.pdf) directly with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission.

When your business model depends on exponentially increasing compute, electricity isn't a line item. It's existential.

Some 20% of planned data center projects are already at risk of delays because the grid simply can't deliver power fast enough.

There are bright spots. Texas — a state whose independent grid has taken more than its fair share of abuse — has deployed [battery storage systems](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southcentral/2026/02/04/856861.htm) to smooth out supply volatility. "This new system, which allows it to use batteries more readily when needed, has improved grid flexibility and stability," Trudi Webster, a spokeswoman for the Electric Reliability Council of Texas, told Insurance Journal. But batteries are a band-aid when demand is growing this fast.

**Our Valley View**

AI companies are spending trillions on infrastructure while largely assuming power supply will keep pace. That bet is looking increasingly shaky. States from New York to Washington are moving to curb data center expansion or shift costs back onto the companies driving them, and residential ratepayers are waking up to the fact that their climbing bills are partially subsidizing Silicon Valley's ambitions. The AI firms that invest early in renewables, grid partnerships and genuine community buy-in will get to keep building. Everyone else may find that the real bottleneck for AI was never compute or chips — it was the plug in the wall.
