GOVERNANCE

# OpenAI calls its own Pentagon deal 'sloppy'

OpenAI signed a deal with the Pentagon. Less than a week later, it's rewriting it.

After Anthropic was [blacklisted from federal contracts](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-comments-secretary-war) last week, OpenAI [stepped in](https://openai.com/index/our-agreement-with-the-department-of-war/) as the Department of War's replacement AI provider — with none of the surveillance safeguards Anthropic had demanded. The backlash came fast.

CEO Sam Altman is now [amending the deal](https://openai.com/index/our-agreement-with-the-department-of-war/) to explicitly ban the use of its AI for domestic mass surveillance, adding references to the Fourth Amendment, the National Security Act of 1947 and the FISA Act of 1978. He defended the Pentagon work internally but admitted the company "shouldn't have rushed" and called the deal "opportunistic and sloppy," according to the [Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/openai-ceo-altman-defends-pentagon-work-to-staff-calls-backlash-really-painful-76d769ec).

The political pressure explains the speed. President Trump [called](https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/116144552969293195) Anthropic's leadership "Leftwing nut jobs" for refusing to loosen safety restrictions, and Defense Secretary Hegseth followed with an unprecedented supply chain risk designation. The implicit message to every other AI lab was hard to miss — but by rushing in without guardrails, OpenAI ended up spotlighting exactly what its own contract was missing, in what [Forbes characterized](https://www.forbes.com/sites/the-prompt/2026/03/03/how-anthropics-kerfuffle-with-the-pentagon-became-a-optics-headache-for-openai/) as "an optics headache."

The pushback came from inside the labs, too. Ninety-six OpenAI employees and 629 Google staffers signed a [joint open letter](https://notdivided.org/) objecting to the pressure campaign: "They're trying to divide each company with fear that the other will give in. That strategy only works if none of us know where the others stand." Google employees are separately [pushing for explicit "red lines"](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/26/technology/google-deepmind-letter-pentagon.html) on military AI, according to The New York Times. This kind of cross-company coordination is new territory.

Users responded, too. ChatGPT saw a 295% surge in app uninstalls on Feb. 28, per Sensor Tower, while Anthropic's Claude climbed to [No. 1 on Apple's US App Store](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/28/anthropics-claude-apple-apps.html) amid a viral #CancelChatGPT campaign. Meanwhile, defense contractors including Lockheed Martin are [pulling Anthropic's AI from classified systems](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/society-equity/defense-contractors-like-lockheed-seen-removing-anthropics-ai-after-trump-ban-2026-03-04/), creating operational disruption across the defense supply chain.

**Our Valley View**

The contract revision is the right call, but the speed of the walk-back is revealing. Consumer trust in AI companies is thinner than the industry likes to admit, and a 295% spike in uninstalls is the kind of number that should make every AI CEO think twice before chasing a government deal without doing the legal homework first. The more consequential development might be the employee letter. When workers at rival companies start coordinating on shared ethical red lines, it gets much harder for any single lab to quietly fold under political pressure. The Pentagon wanted to divide and conquer the AI industry. It may have accidentally united it.

# ChatGPT just got a personality and accuracy overhaul

OpenAI's chatbot had a preachiness problem. The company just owned up to it.

On Tuesday, OpenAI [released GPT-5.3 Instant](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-3-instant/), the latest model powering everyday ChatGPT conversations. The update is less about raw intelligence and more about making the chatbot less annoying to talk to. Hallucinations dropped 26.8% — roughly one in four errors the previous model would have made simply doesn't happen anymore. OpenAI also dialed back what it openly called an "overbearing" personality.

"We heard feedback that GPT‑5.2 Instant would sometimes refuse questions it should be able to answer safely, or respond in ways that feel overly cautious or preachy," the company [said in its announcement](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-3-instant/).

GPT-5.3 Instant addresses the complaints in two ways:

- **Fewer unnecessary refusals.** The model is less likely to reject harmless questions because they touch on a topic that could, in some theoretical universe, be sensitive. Ask about the chemistry of household cleaners and you'll get an answer, not a disclaimer.

- **Less moralizing.** The model "tones down overly defensive or moralizing preambles before answering the question," OpenAI [said](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-3-instant/). Translation: it stops hedging everything with paragraphs of caveats before getting to the point.

Importantly, the [GPT-5.3 Instant system card](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-5-3-instant/) shows safety guardrails around genuinely harmful content haven't changed. The fixes target false positives — moments where overcaution was actively getting in the way of usefulness.

The timing is hard to ignore. With users increasingly migrating to Anthropic's Claude, which [climbed to No. 1 on Apple's US App Store](https://www.businessinsider.com/anthropic-claude-hits-number-one-app-store-openai-chatgpt-2026-2) last week, OpenAI has real incentive to fix whatever was driving people away. For the hundreds of millions of people who use ChatGPT every day, how the chatbot talks to you *is* the product.

**Our Valley View**

The most revealing part of this release isn't the hallucination number. It's that OpenAI publicly called its own model "overbearing." Companies almost never concede a personality problem. That admission tells you how real the user exodus was. If Claude's rise taught OpenAI anything, it's that people don't just want a smart chatbot. They want one they actually enjoy talking to. The race for AI dominance might come down to something deeply human: likability.

# Inside OpenAI's $110B round, investors are hedging

OpenAI just closed the largest private funding round in history. The fine print suggests its biggest backers aren't writing blank checks.

On Friday, OpenAI [announced](https://openai.com/index/scaling-ai-for-everyone/) $110 billion in new funding from SoftBank, Nvidia and Amazon, pushing the company's post-money valuation to roughly $840 billion. The round alone is [more than four times larger](https://www.businessinsider.com/openai-funding-round-4-times-bigger-than-largest-ipo-2026-2) than the biggest IPO ever recorded.

The headline number is staggering. The deal structure is more revealing.

Nvidia's final commitment of $30 billion [came in well below](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/the-100-billion-megadeal-between-openai-and-nvidia-is-on-ice-aa3025e3) the $100 billion it originally floated — a 70% reduction. Amazon committed $50 billion, but significant portions are [tied to milestone triggers](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/AMZN/8-k-amazon-com-inc-reports-material-event-2fc225386aa1.html) — including OpenAI going public or achieving what the deal defines as AGI — with exact definitions heavily redacted. SoftBank anchored the round with $30 billion. And Microsoft, OpenAI's largest existing investor and closest partner, [sat this one out](https://www.wsj.com/tech/openai-raises-110-billion-a2a34d23).

For Amazon, this is as much a cloud play as a financial bet. CEO Andy Jassy said it plainly: "Today, we now have the two largest AI labs who are both significantly betting on Trainium," he [told CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/27/amazon-open-ai-cloud-jassy-altman.html). Getting OpenAI to run workloads on Amazon's custom AI chip would be a direct challenge to Nvidia's dominance in AI compute.

Investors are clearly bullish on AI's potential — less certain about the economics. PitchBook analyst Dimitri Zabelin [told Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/openai-funding-round-4-times-bigger-than-largest-ipo-2026-2) this round secures capital and computing power, but the next test is proving large-scale AI economics can support an $840 billion valuation. For context, Anthropic recently closed a $30 billion round at a [$380 billion valuation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-raises-30-billion-series-g-funding-380-billion-post-money-valuation). Together, the two largest AI labs have raised over $140 billion in weeks — and neither has shown frontier AI can sustain these numbers long-term.

**Our Valley View**

$110 billion is a remarkable war chest. It's also a round full of hedges: Nvidia slashed its commitment by 70%, Amazon tied its check to milestones no one will define publicly, and Microsoft stayed home entirely. The Amazon angle deserves attention — by pulling OpenAI onto Trainium chips, Amazon isn't just writing a check, it's positioning itself to control the compute supply chain that AI labs depend on. The question for OpenAI is no longer whether it can raise capital. It's whether it can prove frontier AI is a sustainable business before the next round of investors shows up with even more strings attached.
