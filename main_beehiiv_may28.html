PRODUCTS

AI is now writing the AI
Share X in

Coding agents were supposed to help developers move faster. Now they're being used to build the next generation of coding agents.

OpenAI quietly published a walkthrough last week of how it used Codex, its own coding agent, to build self-improving tax agents for an enterprise customer. The setup is slightly unsettling: Codex writes the agent, runs it, watches where it fails, and rewrites itself to do better next time. The humans are mostly there to review and steer.

It's not a one-off. Cognition, which just closed a $1 billion round at a $26B valuation, says its coding agent Devin now opens roughly 89% of pull requests across its engineering org. The next version of Devin is, in large part, being written by the current version of Devin.

What's shifting alongside the tooling is what developers actually do all day:

  • The old job: Writing code. The new job: Directing a platform on how to write code, as the CIO of a thousand-developer insurance company put it to Forbes.
  • At most companies, that means telling agents what to build and checking what they produce.
  • At the labs themselves, it means doing that on the very tools that are doing the work — supervising the product as it improves itself.

There are real limits. A recent analysis of AI research agents found they still struggle with the parts of problem-solving that aren't pattern-matching — framing questions, deciding what's worth pursuing, knowing when an answer is wrong. Self-improvement has a ceiling, and nobody knows where it is.

But the trajectory is hard to miss. The tools doing the work are increasingly the tools doing the building, and the people in the loop are doing less of the work and more of the directing.

Into the Valley

The interesting question isn't whether AI can write AI. It clearly can. The real question is what happens to the rate of improvement once the agents are improving themselves. If every iteration is faster than the last because the thing doing the iterating is also getting faster, the gap between the labs running these systems and everyone else stops growing in a straight line. That's the part nobody has fully priced in, and it's why paying $26B for a company whose own engineers barely write code anymore stops sounding quite so crazy.

AI cloned dead pilots' voices. The NTSB pulled the whole docket offline.
Share X in

When planes crash, the federal government releases the transcripts. It does not release the audio. That distinction just stopped meaning anything.

On November 4, 2025, UPS Flight 2976 crashed in Louisville shortly after takeoff. The NTSB investigated, held a public hearing in May, and posted its full docket online — including spectrograms, essentially pictures of the cockpit audio waveform, sitting alongside the written transcript. Within days, users fed those spectrograms into audio reconstruction tools and produced clips of the pilots' final words. The clips spread across social media. By May 21, the NTSB had taken its entire public investigation database offline.

Here's why that's a big deal. Under federal law, the NTSB can't release CVR audio. The agency calls the recordings "highly sensitive", and a committee of airline, manufacturer, FAA, and union reps reviews every transcript before release. The audio stays sealed. The transcript goes public. That bargain has held for thirty-five years — it lets investigators record everything in the cockpit without families ever hearing crew members in their final moments.

What broke it isn't frontier AI. As Thomas Claburn pointed out at The Register, the algorithm that turns a spectrogram back into sound was published in 1984. The NTSB's real mistake was publishing the spectrograms. AI just made the reverse engineering easy enough that someone could do it on a laptop in an afternoon. An NTSB spokesperson told CNN: "Nobody was aware that you can recreate audio from a picture."

Courts are starting to treat AI voice clones as identity misappropriation — a federal judge in New York ruled last July that cloned voices violate state civil rights law, and ElevenLabs faces a separate lawsuit over allegedly training on copyrighted recordings. But that framework is being built for the living. The dead UPS pilots have no plaintiffs, no estates filing complaints. Their voices were reconstructed from federal records the government published itself.

Into the Valley

Locking the docket solves the immediate embarrassment but not the actual problem. The spectrograms have already been downloaded. The reconstructions already exist. The ElevenLabs lawsuits show what happens next, which is that even when a company pulls a voice off its platform, users rebuild it under a different name within days. What aviation safety just learned is that the line between transcript and audio, the one a federal statute spent thirty-five years defending, was holding up only because recovering the audio used to be hard. It isn't anymore. Every agency that built its disclosure rules around the assumption that raw data is safe to publish as long as you don't publish the finished version is about to find out which of those rules quietly stopped working.

RESEARCH

Wharton has a name for what AI is doing to your brain
Share X in

When the AI is wrong, most people go along with it anyway.

A new Wharton paper from researchers Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave put numbers to the problem. In a series of experiments, participants followed AI advice 93% of the time when it was correct — and 80% of the time when it was demonstrably wrong. Even when researchers paid people to think critically and showed them the right answer afterward, more than half still deferred to bad AI advice.

The researchers gave this a name: cognitive surrender. As Nave explained to Wharton's executive education site, it's not the same as offloading math to a calculator — you still decide what to calculate. Cognitive surrender is when the AI makes the judgment and you just sign off. Worse, participants reported feeling more confident when using AI, even when the answers were wrong.

UC Irvine philosophy professor Anastasia Berg sees it playing out in real time: junior workers are skipping the foundational thinking that AI is supposed to assist with, not replace. The tool doesn't just automate tasks — it automates the process through which people learn to do those tasks in the first place.

This lands at an awkward moment for the productivity narrative. Workers using AI are reportedly getting faster, but the broader economy isn't getting more efficient. Wharton's research suggests at least part of the explanation: if people accept wrong AI output half the time, you're not adding productivity — you're just generating errors faster.

Into the Valley

The interesting thing about cognitive surrender is that it doesn't feel like anything. You don't notice the moment you stop checking the AI's work, and the AI doesn't tell you when it's bluffing. The Wharton researchers found that even when people knew the AI could be wrong, even when they were paid to catch mistakes, most of them still didn't. Every productivity story being told about AI right now assumes there's a human in the loop doing the judging. The data suggests that human is mostly nodding along.
